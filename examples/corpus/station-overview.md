# Northwind Fjord Field Station

Northwind Fjord Field Station sits on the north shore of Northwind Fjord, 41 kilometres
inland from the open sea, at the foot of a granite ridge the staff simply call the Wall.
The station was founded in 1987 as a two-room weather hut and has grown into a year-round
research base operated by the fictional Arctic Field Studies Consortium. It is deliberately
small: six staff overwinter, and the summer complement rises to eighteen researchers,
technicians, and visiting students.

## Mission and research programs

The station exists to support long-term observations in four linked programs. The fjord
ecology program samples water chemistry, plankton, and benthic communities at twelve fixed
stations along the fjord. The acoustic wildlife program runs a permanent sensor grid to
study bird and marine mammal activity. The sea-ice program tracks ice thickness and snow
cover from freeze-up in late October to breakup in June. The atmospheric program measures
aerosol chemistry and mercury deposition, with instruments mounted on the roof of Polar Lab.

Data belong to the consortium and are published to its open archive after a twelve-month
embargo. Researchers who collect data at the station are expected to deposit raw files,
calibration records, and a short methods note. The station does not sell access to data and
does not run commercial tours.

## Buildings and daily life

Skjold Hall is the main lodge: twenty-four beds, a communal kitchen, a small infirmary, and
the radio room. Polar Lab next door holds the wet laboratory, the freezer room, and the
atmospheric instrument rack. The powerhouse stands apart from both buildings so that
generator noise and exhaust stay away from the lab. A heated workshop connects the
powerhouse to the fuel depot.

A typical winter day starts with a 07:30 weather and safety check, followed by a shared
breakfast and a 08:15 work plan. Field teams leave in pairs, carry a radio, and report
back every two hours. Dinner is at 18:30. During the dark months the station runs on a
two-shift rota so that someone is always awake in the radio room.

## Climate and access

Annual mean air temperature is minus 6.4 degrees Celsius. February is the coldest month,
with a mean of minus 21 degrees and occasional lows below minus 35. The fjord is usually
ice-covered from late October to early June. Total precipitation is modest, around 320
millimetres water equivalent per year, most of it falling as snow.

Access is seasonal. Between January and April, a chartered ski-equipped plane lands on the
marked strip on the fjord ice twice a week, weather permitting. From June to September the
supply ship MV Nordlys calls roughly every three weeks and lands cargo by crane at the
small pier. May and the second half of October are transition periods with no scheduled
transport. Emergency evacuation is by helicopter from the regional rescue service.

## Staffing

The year-round team includes a station manager, a power and facilities engineer, a
mechanic, a field safety officer, a data manager, and a cook. Summer staff add research
technicians, a medic, and visiting scientists. Everyone, including short-term visitors,
completes the station's safety briefing before moving beyond the marked yard limits.
